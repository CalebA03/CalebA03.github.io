MIT 2.72 Elements of Mechanical Design · Spring '26

../artifacts/lathe/fig1_hero.jpg

Figure 1. Finished lathe.

Introduction

For MIT's 2.72 Elements of Mechanical Design, each team was tasked with designing, modelling, fabricating, and assembling a precision benchtop lathe that can cut 12L14 steel to within 50 microns, survive being dropped from 1.5 m without failure of a critical component, and withstand a hammer impact. Our customer was a materials testing facility needing to produce one ASTM E8 standard sample in under 15 minutes.

From these requirements we derived 26 lathe-level functional requirements covering accuracy, portability, maximum part size, cost, power, safety, material removal rate, noise, maintenance intervals, stiffness, and cutting forces. Subsystem-level FRs were then derived for the spindle, cross slide, z drive, and CNC.

One notable example: the material removal rate FR of 1426 mm/min was derived directly from the customer's 15-minute turnover requirement, giving approximately 10 minutes of cutting time to remove roughly 14,358 mm of material when turning a 20 mm blank down to 12.5 mm over 75 mm. The error budget allocated to each subsystem was derived by estimating subsystem stiffnesses, using free-body diagrams to find the force through each, then scaling allowable precision proportionally to fill the full 50 µm budget.

Spindle

The spindle is constrained by two angular contact bearings. The leftmost bearing is slip-fit to allow axial thermal expansion; the rightmost is press-fit to locate the shaft. Bearings are preloaded by a Belleville washer stack and a Loctite-fixed nut. Key design considerations were thermal runaway, bearing preload (stiffness vs. friction), and avoiding over-constraint.

I performed fatigue analysis of the spindle shaft using the Marin factor method, adjusting the endurance limit of 12L14 steel for machined surface finish, size factor, loading mode, temperature, 95% reliability, and stress concentration factors at fillet radii. The adjusted endurance limit was found to be around 63% above the maximum operating load, confirming no fatigue failure.

I led spindle measurement, developing a plan to verify all FRs. A key correction came at Progress Review 1 when we realised our initial runout measurement used only a single dial indicator, capturing both spindle runout and shaft concentricity error together. We re-measured using the Donaldson and Estler face motion reversal techniques. This dropped our measured runout from 14.6 µm to 1.5 µm, freeing up error budget for other subsystems.

I also measured the runout of a dowel pin in the provided three-jaw chuck using the Donaldson method, obtaining 4.5 thou, well above the precision specification. We therefore ordered a four-jaw chuck, allowing the user to dial in the workpiece to the required precision.

Cross Slide

The cross slide consists of a tool post mounted to a sliding dovetail, driven by a lead screw. The left-hand side is constrained axially (needle bearings) and radially (flanged bushings); the right-hand side is constrained axially only. An early design was over-constrained because the lead screw nut provided an additional radial constraint alongside a right-side bushing, causing lead screw bowing and high hand forces. This was corrected after Progress Review 2 by removing the right-side radial constraint.

I modelled lead screw forces using an FBD of the cross slide. The axial force travels directly through the lead screw. The radial force was calculated by treating the dovetail, bushing, and lead screw as parallel springs, yielding only around 2 N radially. Von Mises stress in the lead screw was calculated to be roughly 35 MPa, well below the approximately 400 MPa allowable.

For bushing selection I evaluated the PV rating (pressure times velocity). At radial loads of around 2 N and speeds of around 160 RPM, bronze sleeve bushings were selected for cost, under $3.

During testing I observed that the Belleville washers were rotating against the flat washers rather than sliding on the flanged bushing. Needle roller bearings were substituted to eliminate this rubbing.

The provided handle had only 50 graduation ticks, giving insufficient resolution for our 0.5 thou precision requirement. I designed a replacement indicator wheel with 100 ticks that also seals the roller bearings.

I also built a homogeneous transformation matrix (HTM) model of the cross slide capturing tool-tip position error from axial lead screw loading, misalignment, thermal expansion, tool post bending, and tool tip deflection.

Z Drive and Tailstock

The z drive uses a similar constraint layout to the cross slide, with radial constraints added to both ends of the tube stock because the carriage rail flexure (CRF) removes the radial constraint that the lead screw nut would otherwise provide.

My contributions were the tailstock modifications, carriage rail flexure, side skirts (in part), CNC motor mounts, and L-bracket fabrication. For the tailstock I completed all CAD and machining: widening the central pocket for the tube stock, drilling and milling side pockets for the guide rods, cutting clamping slits, and adding bolt holes for L-bracket attachment. GD&T was applied specifying guide rod hole and central pocket locations to within 2 thou of nominal.

The four aluminium 6061 L-brackets holding the lathe to the base plate were also designed, waterjetted, and sheet-metal bent by me, sized to withstand both the drop and cutting forces.

Carriage Rail Flexure

The carriage rail flexure (CRF) provides exact constraint to the carriage in the y-direction while remaining compliant in all other directions to avoid over-constraint. The design target was y-direction stiffness of 10 N/µm, derived from the allowable angular deflection of the tool post.

I modelled the CRF as a Timoshenko beam with fixed-guided boundary conditions. System stiffness is a series combination of the CRF, bronze bushing, epoxy, and guide rod, and I tuned the blade width to achieve the target. I chose a bronze sleeve bushing based on PV analysis at the operating load (around 25 lbf) and CNC traverse speed (1.33 ft/min).

To support final dimension selection, I built a MATLAB parametric model sweeping flexure width and length across the feasible design space, plotting peak von Mises stress, y-direction axial stiffness, and x-direction transverse stiffness under worst-case cutting conditions. The nominal design point sits to the right of both the yield and fatigue failure boundaries. SolidWorks FEA of the final 2.5 mm width gave a maximum stress of roughly 60 to 70 MPa at the fillet and a maximum deflection of 0.3 mm.

Fabrication: waterjet cutting the outline after calibrating the machine, drilling and tapping two mounting holes, then bandsaw removal of the frame. The waterjet overcut the fillet on the first attempt even after calibration, and the CRF frame broke during vise clamping. Without the frame I gripped the thicker top face directly. The CRF passed the death cut at Progress Review 3 and all subsequent drop testing without failure.

Powertrain

The powertrain uses a round belt and pulley system covered by a 3D-printed PLA belt safety shield. I designed, modelled, and fabricated the shield. The two governing failure modes were shock loading from the 1.5 m drop requirement and motor heat during continuous operation. Minimum wall thickness was determined from the energy the shield must absorb before failure; vent hole size was determined from the estimated temperature gradient, heat production, and airflow rate.

CNC System

The CNC system uses two NEMA 23 2.4 Nm stepper motors driving their respective lead screws through double diaphragm couplers, powered by GRBL software, off-the-shelf drivers, and an Arduino Uno. I completed component selection for almost all CNC components, derived first-pass CNC FRs, modelled torque requirements, and designed and fabricated the cross-slide CNC motor mounts.

Motor torque requirements were determined by summing the torque for desired angular acceleration of the lead screw, linear acceleration of the carriage, and rotational inertia of the coupling and motor (approximately 0.6 Nm). At around 160 RPM for both axes, I identified a US-based supplier with a motor meeting the requirements at reasonable cost.

The motor mounts were waterjet from 1/8 in Al5052, the centre pocket bored to motor diameter, bolt holes drilled, and the sides sheet-metal bent to 90 degrees. Two lessons: relief cuts are essential inside any feature you intend to bend, and springback must be calculated before bending.

Lessons Learnt

I came into this class with almost no prior machining experience. The biggest lesson: start earlier. I only began machining in the back half of the semester, which cost me time on the spindle and cross slide. Machining to tight tolerances takes longer than expected and rewards time and repetition.

On the content side, machine elements (bearings, lead screws, flexures) were entirely new to me. Getting ahead on lecture content earlier would have helped enormously.

The most important lesson was to slow down and think. Thirty minutes at the whiteboard with a colleague often resolved in one go what I had been stuck on for days.
